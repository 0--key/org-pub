# Introduction     :Introduction:AI_Hype:Need_for_Clarification:Universal_Models_vs_Intelligence:

Good afternoon. It's still too early for us to look into the
future. From the comments, I understood that we still have many
illusions about the present state of universal, cloud-based, global
supermodels and AI that "**knows everything**". Furthermore, there is
little understanding of what is required for us to consider this
system as **intelligence**.

I also contributed to the hype surrounding them, and my old videos
contain a lot of excitement and fear, both reflected and my own. Now,
it is perhaps time to reconcile the singular reality regarding this
revolutionary **generative AI**. I've tried to hint that not everything
is so straightforward, but fragmented reminders, quotation marks, and
phrases like "so-called" are not working. We continue to expect things
from large language models that they, by themselves, cannot yet give
us.

I hope today we will finally figure everything out, well, for a
while. First, we'll recall what modern Artificial Intelligence, at its
core, actually is. This includes neural networks, generative AI, and
large language models (LLMs). Then, we'll determine what inherent and
incurable (at least for now, from within) flaws prevent modern AI from
being considered intelligence and, therefore, from being used for
professional purposes. Next, we'll try to figure out how, despite all
these flaws, AI bots have created such a stir, genuinely caused, and
continue to cause a revolution, attracting, by some estimates,
trillions of dollars in investment.

We will also try to understand how justified the fears regarding
modern AI are, and whether all the risks against which entire
continents are so vigorously insuring themselves are real. Finally,
we'll look at how professional `domain intelligence systems` could be
structured to truly qualify as intelligence, and what role language
models will play in them. So, let's go. The path ahead is long but, in
my opinion, fascinating and not without paradoxes. This is called,
"You wanted a lecture? Well, here it is".


# What we call AI     :LLM_Definition:Statistical_Machine:Linguistic_Reconstruction:

As I've said, the foundation of modern, highly-publicized AI is **Large
 Language Models (LLM)**, based on artificial neural networks.

A reminder:

> an LLM is a massive statistical machine that reads billions of texts
> and learns to predict which word should come next.

Because there are many texts covering almost all areas of human
activity, the model begins to look like it *understands language*. It
can explain, reason, answer questions, write code, and maintain a
dialogue. However, it is **not intelligence**. Inside it, there are no
formalized concepts, no causal model of the world, no goals as
intentions, and no criterion for truth. There is only a statistical
model of language, optimized for a plausible continuation of the text.

It is precisely thanks to this optimized statistical model that the
LLM is capable of appearing reasonable without possessing its own
understanding of reality. And the larger the model, and the more texts
it has learned, the more reasonable it appears. The LLM **does not
reason or calculate** in the human or formal sense. It does not operate
on objects, derive consequences, or verify the truth of its
conclusions.

Instead, it statistically reconstructs the **form of reasoning** and
calculation based on the linguistic patterns in the data on which it
was trained. That's the scientific way of putting it. More simply, the
model **guesses the flow of reasoning and calculation** based on
accumulated statistics. In other words, it doesn't solve a problem; it
generates text that **looks like a solution**, as long as the problem
fits into familiar linguistic patterns.

By its nature, an LLM is **not a reference system or a knowledge base**,
although it can be used as both. It is a <span class="underline">generative language model</span>
that doesn't extract facts but reconstructs plausible formulations,
again, based on language statistics. The model doesn't find knowledge;
it reproduces its textual form *without* an internal criterion for
truth and without a connection to reality. That already doesn't sound
like a professional, does it? And since we've started talking about
the professional application of AI, I will continue to focus on the
topic of creating full-fledged professionals with artificial
intelligence, primarily based on LLMs.


# Inherent flaws in language models     :LLM_Flaws:Lack_of_Explainability:Instability:Hallucination:

LLMs have a multitude of **inherent flaws** that have yet to be
overcome. Importantly, I am discussing internal changes to the
architecture and logic of the models, not external tools that
compensate for these shortcomings.  Despite all internal architectural
ingenuity, modern, even top-tier, models possess all these flaws, some
more than others. Let's review them.


## Lack of Explainability

-   **Complete explainability in LLMs does not exist**. We cannot
    accurately and reliably explain how the model arrived at each
    result.
-   Existing interpretation methods only allow for a fragmentary
    analysis of model behavior, and that is post-factum; they do not
    provide a reproducible explanation of the decision-making logic.
-   Simply put, thanks to the latest research, we have some guesses, but
    only partly. **There is no transparent inference, no formalizable
    proof, no causal tracing of the solution**.
-   What looks like reasoning is its linguistic illusion. As you can
    imagine, this is fatal for a profession.


## Instability and Non-Determinism

-   Language models are **unstable and non-deterministic**. Try asking the
    same question several times, even to the best chatbots, and you will
    get different answers, sometimes contradictory.
-   This is unacceptable in law, medicine, or engineering.


## Lack of Semantic Understanding

-   LLMs **do not understand meaning, only statistics**. This is the main
    root of the problem.
-   The models have no concepts, no rules, no causality, and no
    ontology. They mimic meaning but do not possess it. This makes them
    **logically unreliable**.


## Uncalibrated Confidence (Hallucination)

-   LLMs are capable of textually expressing doubt but **lack calibrated
    confidence and a criterion for reliability**.
-   They can be equally confident when they are right, wrong, or
    inventing, and they cannot distinguish between these states.
-   About a year ago, which is quite a long time in AI terms, I
    experimented with models lying. Many of them lied with \*more
    confidence than they stated something factually substantiated.
-   For professional activity, this is a catastrophe when the author
    himself cannot determine the accuracy of his result.


## Vulnerability to Query Errors

-   LLMs are vulnerable to errors in queries—not just typos, but
    semantic and structural distortions.
-   They may understand a poorly written word but often lose meaning
    when the logic of the query, the data structure, or the task context
    is violated.
-   Implicit assumptions, contradictory formulations, mixing levels of
    abstraction, and poorly expressed constraints can destroy the
    quality of the answer, even from a powerful model. In the real
    world, data is rarely clean, logical, and non-contradictory.
-   It is in such conditions that the level of professionalism becomes
    decisive and cannot be compensated for with AI. I remind you, I am
    talking about the internal properties of LLMs, not external
    architectures designed to combat them.


## Loss of Logical State

-   LLMs **do not guarantee the stability of the logical state of reasoning**.
-   In long chains, they lose the problem's invariants, ignore
    conditions, confuse entities, and mix up the boundaries of
    conclusion applicability.
-   Even with formally preserved context, this is simply unacceptable in
    engineering, finance, medicine, law, and many other fields.


## No Internal Criterion for Truth

-   LLMs have **no internal criterion for truth**. They can imitate
    verification, logic, and doubt, but they do not possess their own
    mechanism for establishing truth.
-   An LLM easily states one thing and, a couple of queries later,
    something contradictory to the first. And they will agree that they
    were wrong and even explain why. Remember that caricature of the AI
    surgeon removing the appendix from the wrong side?
-   Their verification is a style of answer, not an act of
    knowledge. Without external support from verifiable reality, the
    model cannot distinguish between truth and a plausible illusion.


## Lack of Long-Term Memory and Knowledge Management

-   LLMs lack **long-term operational memory and a knowledge management
    system**. They have no mechanisms for fact versioning, auditing,
    cumulative learning, or knowledge updates.
-   In fairness, there are mechanisms for **continuous learning**, but for
    now, they have more problems than achievements.
-   Although there is parametric memory in the weights and short-term
    contextual memory, this is still insufficient for professional
    activity.


## Inherent Hallucination

-   Generative models are inevitably prone to **hallucinations**, and this
    is not a bug but a direct consequence of the architecture. The model
    only sees word probabilities.
-   It creates beautiful text based on statistics, and if the knowledge
    is weak, it fills in the picture. Scaling reduces the frequency of
    hallucinations but does not eliminate them as an architectural
    property.
-   The model still doesn't know where the truth is and where the
    plausible invention lies.

Given the listed properties, universal models can only be \*assistants,
consultants, text analyzers, generation tools, or interfaces to
knowledge bases\*.

In all these cases, their results are subject to careful evaluation
and verification, considering the above-mentioned tendencies.  In
other words, they are helpers and consultants whose results must
**always be carefully checked**.  In their pure form, LLMs cannot be
professionals, doctors, lawyers, engineers, analysts, strategists, or
verifiers. Even if the model externally appears competent, it is **not
a professional entity**.

It does not bear responsibility, does not operate in a professional
environment, and does not possess the concept of an error as a
violation of norms (rather than just an unsuccessful answer).  It is
an imitation of a profession, not its carrier. It's important to
emphasize once again that all this is about universal models **in their
pure form**.  In various hybrids, LLMs have a chance to become a part,
though not always the most important one, of a genuine
**domain-specific intelligence**.


# Why the "statistical machine" revolutionized     :LLM_Revolution:Natural_Computer_Interface:Cognitive_Power:Scalability:

"Well, okay," you might say, "if these super-bots are so unreasonable,
if they have so many fundamental limitations, why are they truly
causing a new technological and even cognitive revolution?"
Especially since other, no less useful, elements of artificial
intelligence have existed for decades, many decades, but they didn't
create a revolution.

The only thing that created a stir was perhaps the symbolic Deep Blue,
which won against the world chess champion.  If I had to summarize it
in one sentence, I believe LLMs have fulfilled a dream of humanity,
one that has existed since the sixties of the last century.  The dream
is about \*communicating with a machine as you would with a person,
using ordinary human language\*—the dream of the notorious **natural
computer interface**.  So, the current revolution is \*not so much a
revolution of artificial intelligence as a revolution in human-machine
communication\*. But not only that.


## The Illusion of Intelligence for the Layperson

-   First and foremost, LLM bots are the first AI that **looks** like
    intelligence to the ordinary person, the non-computer specialist.
-   Expert systems appeared back in the eighties, but they were boring,
    worked in a narrow niche, and couldn't communicate in human
    language.
-   Working with them required ontology engineers, and they looked like
    smart Excel tables.
-   LLMs, for the first time, gave people the **feeling of communicating
    with an artificial mind**. It was a **psychological explosion**, not a
    technological one.
-   LLM is the AI that **doesn't need an interface because it is the
    interface**. This makes it accessible to billions.


## Text-Based Power and Universal Access

-   But LLMs don't just communicate; they **work with text at a level
    comparable to a human**.
-   The good old AI has long been able to classify, assess risk,
    recognize images, diagnose, solve equations, and find patterns. But
    the old man couldn't speak to us in our language.
-   And yet, **text is the main carrier of human knowledge**. When a
    system appeared that could understand text, interaction with the
    computer suddenly moved beyond technology into culture, education,
    business, and communication.
-   Furthermore, LLMs, for the first time, **united access to a wide
    segment of human knowledge in one place**.
-   The expert systems of good old AI worked with a small rule base,
    limited to one industry, and one data type.
-   LLMs, however, work with textbooks, articles, code, forums, history,
    business cases, and fiction. For the first time, LLM began to look
    like a **universal assistant**.
-   Though it's an illusion, the scale of the illusion was and remains
    enormous.


## Scalability and Investor Interest

-   Unlike the good old AI, LLMs **scale well**.
-   Expert systems are difficult to expand and expensive to
    maintain. They break when new rules are added and require enormous
    manual labor.
-   LLM, in terms of scaling, is a true phenomenon.
-   You simply increase the model, and the quality grows. You add data,
    and the capabilities grow. You increase the context, and the scope
    of application grows.
-   This is a scaling mechanism that looks very promising at first
    glance. And investors and corporations love such obvious prospects.
-   The revolutionary role was also played by the fact that the LLM
    appears to be a **universal tool**, and these are what capture
    markets, create ecosystems, and form industries.


## Hype and Automation of Cognitive Routine

-   The flaws of language models appear solvable to non-specialists. And
    developers were quick to promise investors they would solve them.
-   As a result, a \*deliberate and targeted hype was created, and
    trillions of dollars flowed into the AI industry.
-   Business also saw the prospect of **automating cognitive routine**,
    and executives of all ranks saw an opportunity to create an
    objective consultant.
-   And generally, if you don't delve into the details, they create the
    appearance that they are smarter than a human, which is intriguing
    and sparks a desire to check if they truly are that smart.
-   When unprepared people rushed to communicate with chatbots, it
    genuinely seemed to them that they were communicating with
    intelligent entities. This inflated the hype even more.
-   For the first time in history, \*AI became a mass product on
    everyone's lips.
-   Of course! It answers confidently, reasons like a person, creates
    texts, jokes, writes code, and conducts a dialogue. This is a
    powerful cognitive effect.
-   The brain automatically attributes intelligence to language
    models. Although architecturally this is false, the \*appearance of
    intelligence creates and amplifies the revolutionary effect.
-   And although all the aforementioned non-intelligent properties are
    fully present, people want to believe in a miracle.
-   And this will continue until they genuinely need a professional—not
    a talking medical handbook, but a doctor; not a legal knowledge
    base, but a lawyer to whom they can entrust, forgive the dramatic
    word, their destiny.
-   These were my answers to two important questions: Why LLMs cannot be
    professionals, and why, despite all their properties, they sparked
    the AI revolution.
-   Looking from a slightly different angle, the essence and cause of
    the AI revolution can be summarized as: **The revolution is not in
    reliability, but in the scale of application**.
-   The revolution is that LLMs gave ordinary people access to
    **cognitive power**.
-   The revolution is in **speed**. LLMs do in minutes what used to take
    months or days.
-   The revolution is that the LLM is a \*universal interface to all
    digital systems.
-   Errors are not an obstacle because errors can be filtered, and the
    benefit is scalable.
-   The revolution is because LLMs provide value even with
    unreliability, as they allow for the **automation of cognitive
    labor**, not physical labor.

# Risks of language models     :AI_Risks:Human_Intent_Multiplier:Weaponized_Intent:

Now let's talk about the risks of developing and scaling universal
language models. Are these risks real, or are they also invented, like
the intelligence itself?  Should people fear the uncontrolled
development of AI, as is feared, for example, in Europe? Why are
advanced models being tested by special \*Red Teams to avoid the
escalation of risks?  It means the risks do exist. The answer is
simple: Language models are dangerous \*not as intelligence, but as a
tool that affects people, processes, and systems.  It's important to
understand that LLMs are not dangerous as a **subject**; they are
dangerous as a powerful **amplifier of human capabilities**, both good
and bad.  LLMs are dangerous not for their reasonableness, but for
their **speed, scale, and accessibility**.  Any tool that is accessible
to billions, works instantly, generates convincing text, can
manipulate emotions, create fakes, write malicious code, and scale the
actions of malicious actors.  Such a tool creates a **mass social
risk**. It is not intelligence; it is a **mega-powerful information
amplifier**, like a press for meanings.  I like the concept of an
amplifier, not just informational, but **cognitive**.  Red Teams check
not the **will of the AI, but the \*potential for abuse**.  For example,
a Red Team searches for answers to questions like:

-   Can the model be used to obtain **bio-recipes**?
-   Can filters be bypassed to generate **propaganda for violence**?
-   Can socially divisive content be **automatically generated**?
-   Can it write **exploits**?
-   Can an \*autonomous agent that operates without control be created?
-   Can the generation of text be used to **manipulate mass beliefs**?
-   Can LLMs be used for **targeted persuasion**?

In other words, it's all about **people abusing the tool**, not about a
machine uprising.


## Lowering Professional Barriers

-   Interestingly, LLMs create risks because they give
    **non-professionals access to professional actions**.
-   Moreover, one person can now do what previously required a team of
    those very professionals.
-   As my grandmother used to say, "God does not give horns to a butting
    cow." But here you are, with horns of your choice.
-   Anyone can, if they wish, generate bio-instructions, write exploit
    code without prior knowledge, create fake political campaigns, forge
    speeches, documents, videos, create plausible deceptive mailings,
    and manipulate people through message chains.
-   LLMs have \*removed the entry barriers to fields where professionals
    used to work. So, LLMs have potentially become a weapon in the hands
    of any bad person.


## Harm to Users

-   However, they can also directly harm those who use them. Their
    errors look plausible, and thus the model can give incorrect medical
    advice, invent a non-existent legal norm, generate a false analysis,
    fake a quote, or convince a person of an incorrect conclusion.
-   Remember that famous case where a chatbot advised a sick person to
    commit suicide?


## Systemic Risk in Autonomous Chains

-   A completely different level of danger is that LLMs can be
    integrated into **autonomous agent chains**.
-   LLMs are passive by themselves. But if they are embedded in robots,
    drones, financial systems, decision-making chains, infrastructural
    systems, or uncontrolled agents, the same inherent errors lead to a
    \*systemic risk that an LLM error will result in dangerous system
    actions.


## Weaponized Human Intent Multiplier

-   LLMs are dangerous as **amplifiers of human evil** — or, as they say
    in the West, a *"weaponized human intent multiplier"*.
-   They are not evil themselves, but they make evil cheaper, faster,
    more massive, and more accessible.
-   These are not horror stories; this is an understanding of the risks
    that a powerful tool can become a weapon, and therefore these risks
    need to be classified and mitigated in every possible way.
-   And, as usual, people kill, not weapons. (I say this as a fan of
    small arms and an experienced shooter). Although, weapons configured
    to kill without human involvement already exist. (I've already
    talked about this).

Well, I think I've thoroughly defeated universal large language
models, which we call AI, as a potential professional tool. Or have I?
Or have I just confused everything?  Well, the narrative turned out to
be so multi-layered. Despite the flaws that make modern AI
professionally unfit, it caused a revolution, became a universal
interface, and gave rise to serious risks. That's the two-word
summary.


# Real-world domain-specific intelligence systems     :Domain_Specific_AI:Hybrid_Systems:

Since we were originally talking about domain intelligence, do real
\*domain-specific AI systems exist today?  Can LLMs be used in a way
that neutralizes their properties unsuitable for professional
systems?  In principle, yes. But the role of the LLM in such systems
might turn out to be small.

Here is a table with several examples of professional AI systems for
different industries.  I won't talk about all of them. You can search
by name and read more if something interests you.  The main point is
that in many of these systems (though not all), such revolutionary
language models, if present, are what they essentially are: **a means
of communication in human language**.

They are an interface to the knowing, thinking, problem-solving,
 verifying, evaluating, and other cognitive components that existed
 long before generative models. But today, they are integrated into
 modern AI systems as an architectural core.  All these components
 appeared decades ago as part of the "good old AI" ecosystem. Now,
 they have been brought to light, adapted to work with each other and
 with generative AI, and an attempt is being made to collectively
 eliminate the flaws that prevent bots from becoming professionals.
 This means almost all professional systems are **hybrids or even old
 architectures \*without any LLMs**.


# Hybrid variants involving LLM     :Hybrid_Architectures:Neuro_Symbolic_Systems:Knowledge_Graphs:

Just for information, I will list some of the \*hybrid architectures
currently being used to try and create domain intelligence.


## 1. Neuro-Symbolic Systems

-   **LLM + Formal Logic (Reasoning Engine)**. In this case, a world model
    is added to the LLM.
-   These provide what LLMs fundamentally lack: strict logical
    conclusions, correctness verification, interpretability,
    non-contradictory rules, and a causal structure.
-   In such a hybrid, the LLM provides **query interpretation and answer
    generation**. The symbolic part does the rest: checking reasoning,
    rules, norms, and inference steps in the legal, medical, or
    technical domain.
-   This provides **real reliability**. This is currently the main
    candidate for practical domain AI.
-   However, as far as I know, there are great difficulties in combining
    LLMs with the symbolic subsystem, and full-fledged world models are
    also quite difficult to create.


## 2. Knowledge Graph and Ontology Integration

-   **LLM + Knowledge Graph and Ontology (Graph RAG and Concept Graphs)**.
-   This is promising because domain intelligence is impossible without
    **structured concepts and relationships**. Graphs and ontologies
    provide exactly that: clear concepts, relationships between them,
    constraints, typing, and checks.
-   Here, the LLM provides the language but does not create the
    knowledge structure. The graph does that. As a result, the hybrid
    gains a \*semantic foundation that a pure LLM lacks.


## 3. External Long-Term Memory

-   **LLM + External Long-Term Memory (Episodic + Semantic Memory)**.
-   LLMs are trained on a static dataset and by default do not learn
    from every new case. They do not update their knowledge in real-time
    or maintain a conscious history of accumulated experience.
-   Domain intelligence must store knowledge, update it, link new
    information to old, version it, and accumulate experience.
-   Hybrid memory (vector DB, episodic logs) transforms the LLM from a
    talking Excel spreadsheet into an **interactive system with
    accumulating experience**.


## 4. Program of Solved Execution (PoSE)

-   **LLM + Program of Solved Execution (PoSE/SoL)**. I'm not sure if this
    needs translation.
-   As we know, LLMs are poor at calculating, evaluating, and drawing
    conclusions. But they excel at \*generating code that can be executed
    by external mathematical solvers, CAD systems, simulators, proof
    verification tools, and optimizers.
-   The logic of the hybrid is: the model generates the reasoning or
    program, the solvers execute it, and then the result is
    checked. This provides **guaranteed correctness of calculations,
    repeatability, and eliminates guessing**.
-   Simply put, the LLM is the interface, and the solver is the engine
    for formal inference.


## 5. Multi-Step Reasoning Pipelines

-   **Multi-step reasoning pipelines (Self-Check, Self-Critic,
    Multi-Agent)**.
-   Such hybrids compensate for the fundamental errors of the LLM by
    adding \*self-checking, aggregation of multiple reasoning
    trajectories, search for inconsistency, iterative correction, model
    voting, and distribution of cognitive load\*.
-   This provides a **quasi-logic** unavailable to a pure LLM and greatly
    improves reliability in analytical domains.


## Sum Up

However, it must be admitted that it still does not solve the problem
of concepts, remaining only part of the puzzle. As you can see,
hybrids **treat the symptoms but do not change the nature of the
LLM**. They add logic, memory, rules, graphs, and agents, but they do
not create a complete intelligence architecture. This is currently the
case because the internal architecture of the LLM is constantly
evolving precisely with the goal of combating known flaws.
Furthermore, hybrid architectures are not just "crutches" but a
full-fledged and powerful direction of development. However, a
complete architecture of domain intelligence, one that combines
universality, specialization, and verifiability, **does not yet
exist**. Again, "yet".


# "Back to the future?"     :Architectural_Impasse:Future_of_Domain_Systems:Personal_AI:

Now that we have recalled what we are dealing with and why, we can
return to contemplating the future. Yes, in some industries—medicine,
law, and finance—domain AI exists. But these are **superstructures over
a language model**, not new forms of intelligence. Behind the facade of
industrial solutions are the same bots, just more expensive, stricter,
and legally protected. These systems have entered the market but **have
not escaped the architectural impasse**.

Therefore, their market entry is incomplete. This does not create a
basis for creating all domain intelligence. And now we will return to
the domain systems of the future and, without delving into the
